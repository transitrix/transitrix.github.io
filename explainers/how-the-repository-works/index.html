How an Enterprise Architecture Repository Works

A practical guide based on the Transitrix Methodology

One repository, and everything else comes out of it

Your architecture lives in a folder, in files a person can read. Not in a tool, not in a database, not in a hundred diagrams that stopped agreeing with each other two years ago.

Everything below is about that folder: what goes where, who may put it there, and what you get back.

What you already have

Interviews. A spreadsheet someone emailed. A slide deck from a project that finished. Notes from a meeting where three people described the same process differently.

Keep the original file and its provenance. Where a supported converter is configured, Office documents, PDFs and HTML can be converted into a readable working form. Check conversion failures and uncertain extraction before using the result. Markdown and plain text can be retained directly. A converted document is still source material; conversion does not establish truth or approval.

field/ — what you were told

Raw material, exactly as it arrived. An interview stays an interview. A draft stays a draft.

Two people may contradict each other here, and that is not a problem to fix: this folder records what was said, not what is true. What matters is knowing who said it and when, so that anything built on it can be traced back.

codex/ — what you must obey

Laws, regulations, industry standards — and your own policies, which count just as much.

External authorities issue laws and regulations; your organisation also issues its own authority documents. Preserve the issuing source faithfully, with its status and effective dates. Do not rewrite an authority document to make the model appear compliant. Use it to identify which obligations apply.

knowledge/ — what someone worked out

The step in between. Someone reads five interviews and a policy, works out what they actually mean together, and writes that down — with the sources cited and the date on it.

It is not raw material any more, because a person has made sense of it. It is not the model yet, because nobody has admitted it. Think of it as a good, careful answer to one question, kept where the next person can find it instead of working it out again.

Many teams never need this folder. Keep it if your material arrives faster than you can model it; skip it if it does not.

canon/ — what the organisation says is true

The model itself. One thing per file: a process, an application, a goal, a requirement, each with its own file, its own type, and the dates between which it holds.

This is the admitted architecture model. Source statements do not become model facts merely because they were collected or analysed. Authority documents in codex/ retain their own force; admission to canon/ is a separate question.

The gate: a tool proposes, a person admits

A tool can read your material and propose entries at any hour of the night. Nothing it proposes enters the model on its own.

Open a pull request so the team's authorised human reviewer can inspect the change and its evidence. Validation is not approval. Admit the change only after the required review, checks and merge, and record who ran the admission gate. The pull request is the review surface; opening one alone does not admit a candidate.

operations/decisions/ — why you did it that way

Your team's own record of the choices it made: what was decided, when, and what the alternative was. One file per decision.

These sit beside the model rather than inside it, and the distinction does real work. A decision is a fact about your team. A weakness in the business you are modelling is a fact about the business, and belongs in the model.

What comes out

Derived diagrams, documents and reports answer questions about the model. A capability map, a layered view, a process diagram — three different questions asked of the same content. View definitions live in views/, alongside canon/. Use the renderer that supports the selected view or document format.

Check the source revision and regenerate configured views when their sources change. Keep both the model and the view definitions: those are the inputs needed to reproduce a projection. An exported picture or PDF is a snapshot and can become stale; it does not promise automatic refresh.

And one repository is not the boundary

Each project can keep its own repository, model and decisions. A shared decision index can help teams read choices across projects while retaining the source repository and revision.

Where read-only collection is configured, verify which repositories it can access, what it collected and when it last ran. Collection does not grant write access to another team's repository. Rebuilding an index requires the retained source decisions and collection configuration; a schedule or automatic collection must be established explicitly.

Explore the structure in the public example enterprise repository, or create your own model.
